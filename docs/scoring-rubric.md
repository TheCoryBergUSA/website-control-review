# Website Control Review scoring rubric

## Purpose

This rubric evaluates whether a business website gives a visitor a clear reason to act. It uses a customer-first messaging approach inspired by the questions a prospective customer asks on a first visit.

The rubric is not a substitute for user research or conversion testing. It identifies clarity risks worth addressing.

## First-fold test

Evaluate the desktop and mobile first screen before assessing the rest of the page. A first-time visitor should be able to answer these questions without scrolling:

1. What does this business help me do?
2. Is this for someone like me?
3. What problem does it solve?
4. What result can I expect?
5. What should I do next?

If a question cannot be answered from visible text and calls to action, the report should state that directly and cite the visible evidence.

## Scoring scale

Score each dimension from 0 to 3.

| Score | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | Absent, contradictory, or materially confusing. |
| 1 | Present but vague, buried, or difficult to connect to the visitor. |
| 2 | Clear with a minor gap or avoidable ambiguity. |
| 3 | Specific, immediately understandable, and aligned with the next step. |

The total score is a diagnostic aid, not the customer-facing outcome. Prioritize the three largest clarity risks rather than presenting a generic grade.

## Dimensions

### 1. Audience and offer clarity

Can a visitor quickly identify who the business helps and what it offers?

- Evidence: hero heading, subheading, navigation labels, service labels.
- Common failure: a slogan describes values but never names the service or audience.

### 2. Customer problem

Does the site name the practical or emotional problem the visitor wants solved?

- Evidence: first-fold copy, problem section, service descriptions.
- Common failure: the site talks about the company before acknowledging the customer.

### 3. Desired outcome

Does the site show the better result the customer can expect?

- Evidence: outcomes, transformations, benefits, examples, testimonials.
- Common failure: feature lists without a clear business or personal result.

### 4. Primary call to action

Is there one obvious, specific action a ready visitor can take?

- Evidence: primary button label, location, repetition, form destination.
- Common failure: several equally weighted actions or a vague label such as "Learn more."

### 5. Plan and reduced uncertainty

Does the site explain what happens after the visitor acts?

- Evidence: process steps, expectations, timing, onboarding details.
- Common failure: a visitor must contact the business before knowing how engagement works.

### 6. Guide and trust

Does the business demonstrate enough empathy and authority to be trusted?

- Evidence: specific expertise, examples, reviews, credentials, case studies, practical guidance.
- Common failure: generic claims such as "we care" without proof or useful detail.

### 7. Success and cost of inaction

Does the site make success tangible and acknowledge what the visitor avoids by acting?

- Evidence: before-and-after outcomes, risk reduction, transformation language.
- Common failure: urgency relies on hype instead of a credible consequence.

### 8. Message focus and friction

Does the page maintain one coherent message and next step?

- Evidence: competing audiences, offer count, navigation, popups, form complexity, visual hierarchy.
- Common failure: the hero tries to serve multiple audiences or asks visitors to interpret jargon.

## Technical evidence, scored separately

Technical checks should support the business review but must not be mixed into the clarity score.

- Core performance evidence
- Mobile layout evidence
- Basic accessibility evidence
- Basic on-page SEO evidence
- Contact and lead-path checks
- Technology ownership questions that require human confirmation

## Recommendation standard

Each recommendation must include:

1. Priority: highest, important, or later.
2. Finding: one clear sentence.
3. Evidence: exact visible copy, a tool result, or an unavailable-check note.
4. Why it matters: the likely visitor decision affected.
5. Suggested change: a practical next action.
6. Classification: measured, observed, inferred, or unavailable.

## Prohibited report behavior

- Do not claim that a page will increase revenue or conversions.
- Do not diagnose legal compliance or security from a surface scan.
- Do not criticize visual taste without connecting it to clarity, trust, readability, or task completion.
- Do not state that a business lacks ownership of its technology unless a human has confirmed account ownership.
